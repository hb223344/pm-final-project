Slide 5 · Strategy

Stop being one more swappable option

Problem hook

We must stop Riverty from being just one more swappable option at checkout, by fixing consumers’ stress of tracking scattered pay-later bills and not being able to pay later in store.

Case: BU Pay & Credit, Riverty’s First Consumer Card · Persona: Lena, 32, heavy pay-later user

Value proposition

One Riverty screen that shows everything Lena owes on her card and her pay-later orders, with each due date clearly listed, so payday never brings surprises.

Why now: Klarna and PayPal are already in Lena’s wallet, and activity drops right after the first bill (77.6% → 66.3% → 51.0%). If Riverty stays one more app to check, it stays easy to swap out.

Data-backed hypothesis

I believe that B3, one balance view for card and pay-later orders, for heavy pay-later users like Lena, will keep them using the card after their first bill arrives. I’ll measure this as a +5 percentage point increase in the share of heavy users who make at least one purchase in the month after their first bill (baseline 66.3%), within 12 weeks, with a 50/50 split at p < 0.05. We will protect partner merchant share of card volume (no more than 3 points below control) and on-time repayment (no more than 2 points below control).

Slide 6 · Research

Today, Lena does the maths herself

Workaround & competitive set
Klarna appPayPal appRiverty app · 2 screensBank app (assumed)Reminder emails / texts
  1. Before payday, she opens each pay-later app to see what’s due.
  2. In the Riverty app, she checks the card balance, then the pay-later orders, and adds them up in her head.
  3. She compares two reminders from the same week to work out what’s due when.
  4. When the numbers don’t match, she asks support which total is the real one.
Core frustration

The moment she sees two different totals in the Riverty app and two reminders in the same week for different amounts, and can’t tell what she actually owes.

Evidence

“I could not see in one place what I owe on the card and what I owe on my normal pay later orders.” (N-07)

Backed by ticket T-103: participants ask which total is the real one.

Hesitate

Two reminders arrive in the same week for different amounts (T-104). She can’t tell which total is the real one (N-07, T-103).

Bounce · moment of misery

She gives up, asks support, or checks her other pay-later apps. The card becomes one more thing to check (N-02), and she slowly stops using it after her first bill.

Solution intervenes · at Scan

One Balance View shows one total, every item with its due date, and the next payment at the top. No adding up, no guessing.

PRD · finalized Must-Haves
  • One total owed, combining the card balance and all Riverty pay-later orders
  • Every unpaid item with shop, amount, due date and a “Card” or “Pay later” label
  • The next due date and amount shown at the top
  • Total and next due date update right away when an item is paid
PRD · made explicit

The total has to match the item list to the cent, and if it doesn’t, the app shows an error instead of a wrong number. A wrong total is exactly Lena’s moment of misery.

Prototype · revealed a gap

The PRD never said how card purchases get paid. New rule: card items are paid together as one statement, and pay-later orders are paid one by one.

Slide 8 · Validation

One change, one number, one read date

Hypothesis

I believe that B3, one balance view for card and pay-later orders, for heavy pay-later users like Lena, will keep them using the card after their first bill arrives, measured as a +5 percentage point increase in the share of heavy users who make at least one purchase in the month after their first bill.

Control (A)

Today’s app. The card balance and regular pay-later orders are on two separate screens, and Lena has to open both and add the totals herself.

Variant (B) · exactly one change

One screen showing a single total owed (card plus pay-later orders), a list of each item with its amount and due date, and the next due date highlighted at the top.

Slide 10 · Story

What I learned along the way

What was hard

Being ruthless. Every feature felt important until I asked one question: can Lena still see what she owes without it? Cutting B4 hurt, because it was part of my own value proposition.

What surprised me

How often the AI sounded right while being wrong. It turned one merchant quote into “all merchants” and made up a cause-and-effect. I had to check its work against the data every time.

The aha moment

When the numbers met the interviews. Lena’s frustration was real, but the data showed when it matters: right after the first bill. That turned a general complaint into a clear problem of keeping users, not winning them.

Key takeaways
  • Start with one person and one moment, not a feature list.
  • Building a quick prototype finds gaps that writing a PRD misses. Mine showed I never defined how card payments actually work.
  • Run the maths early. My test needs about 2,700 new heavy users, so it can’t run in the pilot.
What I’d do next
  • Talk to 5–8 real heavy users (not colleagues) to check the first-bill drop-off.
  • Set the missing baselines: heavy-user retention and “which total?” support tickets.
  • Start the approval process for B8 (one due date) now, since it takes months.
